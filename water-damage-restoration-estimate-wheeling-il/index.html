What a Water Damage Estimate Should Include in Wheeling, IL

Two estimates for the same wet basement can look nothing alike. Knowing what a good one contains lets you compare them fairly and spot gaps before work starts.

Why estimates vary so much

Water damage is not a fixed product. One company may bid a quick extraction and a few fans, while another bids full demolition and a week of drying. Both could be reasonable, depending on what the water did. The difference is usually in assumptions, not in honesty.

That is why a written scope matters as much as the total. A scope tells you what the number buys. Without it, you are comparing totals that describe different jobs.

What a good estimate lists

LineWhat it should sayWhy it matters
Water categoryClean, gray or contaminatedSets the safety steps and what can be saved
Affected areaRooms and approximate square footageDrives equipment and labor
ExtractionMethod and areaShows what is removed first
EquipmentType, count and daysThe biggest variable in drying
DemolitionWhat is removed and whereDefines what is replaced later
Cleaning and treatmentSurfaces and productsAvoids vague wording
DisposalDebris removal included or notOften forgotten
ExclusionsRepairs and rebuild not includedPrevents surprises

Not every estimate has every line, but a good one has most of them. If a line is missing, ask whether it is included, excluded or not needed.

Equipment days and daily readings

Drying equipment is usually billed per unit per day. That means a ten-day job with six machines costs far more than a three-day job with two. Ask how many days the estimate assumes and how the crew will decide when to stop. The answer should be readings, not a calendar guess.

Written daily readings let you see why equipment was added or removed. The structural drying page explains the equipment and how the numbers guide the job.

What changes after the estimate

Water finds new places. Opening a wall can reveal wet insulation, and lifting carpet can show wet subfloor. A fair estimate says what happens if the scope grows, and who must approve the change. Ask for revisions in writing, with a short explanation.

Red flags

  • A lump sum with no detail
  • Pressure to sign on the spot
  • A quote by phone without seeing the loss
  • No mention of readings or documentation
  • Cash only or large upfront payment demands
  • Vague "full restoration" with no scope

We do not quote a number over the phone without seeing the loss, because two wet rooms can be very different jobs. Scope and price are explained before work starts.

Comparing estimates fairly

Line them up side by side. Mark where the scopes differ, such as one including demolition and the other not. Ask each company to explain its assumptions. The lowest number is rarely the best deal if it leaves out steps you will need to pay for later.

A Wheeling note

If you hire a public adjuster, the Illinois Department of Insurance says the contract can only be canceled by certified mail within five business days after it was signed. Read any contract fully before signing, including estimates from restoration companies.

For permit questions that affect repair costs, the Village of Wheeling Community Development Department is at (847) 459-2620, and it lists water heater installation, plumbing work and electrical work among permit-required jobs.

Where to start

Describe the loss to each company the same way, and ask for the same level of detail. For a basement loss, compare how each handles carpet and drywall, and read basement water damage cleanup first. Towns near Wheeling such as Mount Prospect and Lincolnshire see the same range of bids.

Questions to ask before anyone visits

  • What caused the water and how long ago?
  • Is the water clean, gray or contaminated?
  • Which rooms and floors are wet?
  • Is anyone at risk from electrical hazards?
  • Has the water source been stopped?

A company that asks these questions is trying to understand the loss. Expect a phone call to produce advice and a visit to produce an estimate. Be wary of a firm number from a short call alone.

How the estimate and the insurance claim interact

Your insurer prepares its own estimate, and it may differ from the restoration estimate. That is normal. Differences usually come from what each party saw and when. Share your documents with the adjuster, ask for a copy of the insurer's estimate and compare line by line. Ask your insurer how it handles differences.

If the claim is disputed, the Illinois Department of Insurance says to check your policy for an appraisal clause, and other options include a lawyer or a public adjuster.

What happens at the site visit

A crew member walks the property, takes photographs and uses meters and a thermal camera to map the wet areas. They ask about the timeline and the source. Then they explain what they found, what must be done first and how long drying is likely to take. You should leave the visit knowing the plan, even before you see the written estimate.

Ask them to point out the dry reference areas, since those become the baseline for the job.

Common questions

Is an estimate the final price?

Not always. If the scope grows, the price can change, and the change should be written and approved.

Will my insurer accept your estimate?

Insurers make their own decisions. We provide documents, and we never promise approval.

Should I choose the cheapest estimate?

Compare scopes first. A low number may leave out steps that you will need to pay for later.

How long should an estimate take?

It depends on the loss. A crew should inspect, take readings and then explain the scope in writing.
