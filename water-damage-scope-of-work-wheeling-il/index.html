How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Wheeling, IL

A scope of work is the document that says what will be done, where and to what standard. Reading it carefully protects you, your insurer and the crew.

What a scope of work is

A scope of work lists the tasks a contractor will perform, in order, with the area and the materials involved. It is different from an estimate, which attaches prices. A good scope can be read by someone who was not at the site and still make sense.

The scope also sets the finish line. It says when a phase is complete, how that will be checked and who signs off. Without it, disagreements about whether the job is done tend to turn into arguments.

The phases of a typical water scope

1

Emergency services

Stopping the source, extraction and protecting contents.

2

Drying

Setting equipment, monitoring daily and removing it when readings match a baseline.

3

Demolition

Removing materials that cannot be dried, with limits and locations written.

4

Cleaning

Cleaning and treating remaining surfaces.

5

Repair

Rebuilding, painting and flooring, listed separately.

Each phase should have its own list of tasks. If a phase is missing, ask whether it is excluded or part of another company's work.

Quantities and locations

Look for specifics. Square footage, linear feet of drywall to be removed, the height of a flood cut and the rooms involved should be written down. Phrases like "as needed" are a warning. Ask for a number or a range, and ask what triggers a change.

  • Which rooms and surfaces
  • How much material is removed
  • How high a flood cut goes
  • What equipment and for how long
  • What is protected and how

Standards and documentation

A scope should name the standard the crew follows. For water damage, that is ANSI/IICRC S500, and for mold it is S520. You can ask any company whether its crews work to them. The scope should also state what documentation you will receive, such as daily moisture readings, photographs and a closeout summary.

Our crews provide written records of work performed, including moisture readings. These support insurance review and give you a clear history of the job.

Exclusions and assumptions

Every scope has limits. Plumbing repair, roof work, electrical work and permits are usually separate. Mold found later, hidden damage and changes in the loss can change the scope. A clear scope lists what is excluded and how changes are handled, so there are fewer surprises.

Sign-off and change orders

Sign only after you understand each line. When the scope changes, ask for a written change order that states what changed, why and what it costs. A verbal approval is easy to forget. Keep copies in your claim folder.

A Wheeling note

Wheeling lists water heater installation, plumbing work and electrical work as permit-required jobs, and the Community Development Department at (847) 459-2620 answers questions about other items. If your scope includes any of these, ask who is responsible for the permit.

Your scope should also note whether a plumber or electrician must clear a circuit or a line before drying begins.

Using the scope in a claim

Send your insurer the scope, the photographs and the daily readings. Do not expect it to match the adjuster's estimate line for line. Differences are normal and can be resolved by comparing documents. For help with documents, see water extraction. Homes in Prospect Heights and Lincolnshire should keep the same folder.

A short example in plain words

Imagine a laundry room supply line that failed and wet the room and the hallway. A readable scope might say it in steps. Stop the water. Extract standing water from the laundry room and hallway. Remove wet baseboards and the lower two feet of drywall in the laundry room. Set air movers and one dehumidifier and take readings daily. Remove equipment when readings match the dry baseline, then provide a written closeout with photos.

Notice that it names the rooms, the height of the cut, the equipment and the end condition. You can check each item against what you see on site.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • What is the finish condition for each phase?
  • Who decides when equipment can be removed?
  • What happens if more damage is found?
  • Is disposal included?
  • What documents will I receive at the end?

Write the answers on the scope itself, or ask the company to add them. Keep one signed copy in your claim file.

Common gaps in scopes

The gaps we see most are missing exclusions, vague quantities and no mention of documentation. Another is a scope that lists drying but not what happens if readings stay high. Ask what the next step would be and how the cost changes. Good scopes answer these before they are asked.

Another gap is storage. If your belongings are moved for drying, the scope should say where they go, who is responsible for them and how they are returned. Ask for an inventory before items leave the house.

Keeping the scope useful

Store the signed scope with your photos, receipts and readings. If you change contractors, hand the new company the scope and the readings so they know where the job stands. This avoids repeated work.

Common questions

Who writes the scope?

The restoration company writes it after an inspection. You approve it, and your insurer may review it.

Can the scope change?

Yes, when new damage is found. Ask for a written change order.

Does the scope include repairs?

Sometimes. Ask whether rebuilding is in the same scope or a separate phase.

Do I need a scope for a small leak?

A short written scope is still useful. It records what was done and when.
